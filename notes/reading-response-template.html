Reading response

One per assigned paper, due before the lecture that covers it. Four questions, about 400 words total. This is not a summary. We have all read the paper.

Due before lecture Length ~400 words Submit Canvas Weight 15% across the term

The four questions

Each one is asking for a judgement, not a fact you can copy out of the paper. The contrasts below are the difference between a response that earns full credit and one that reads like an abstract with the sentences reordered.

01The claim

What is this paper asserting that was not already established? One or two sentences, in your own words, stated so that someone could disagree with it.

Weak“The paper presents a method for real-time dense SLAM.” That is the topic, not the claim. Nobody could disagree with it.
StrongNames the specific thing that changes, and what it is being claimed against. A claim with no implicit rival is usually a topic in disguise.
02The evidence

Which experiment actually carries the claim, and how far does it carry it? Not every result in the paper carries weight; most tables are context for one or two that matter.

WeakRestating the headline number. The result is not the same thing as the evidence for the claim, and copying the table tells us nothing about whether you believe it.
StrongIdentifies the one comparison that would collapse the claim if it went the other way, and says whether that comparison isolates the thing being claimed or confounds it with something else.
03The weakest assumption

Where does the argument rest on something that might not hold? Name one, and say what breaks downstream if it fails.

WeakQuoting the paper's own Limitations section. The authors already know about those; finding them is not analysis.
StrongSomething the paper treats as so obvious it never argues for it: often an assumption about the data, the scene, or the evaluation protocol rather than about the method.
04What you would run next

One experiment. Say what you would measure and what result would change your mind. That last clause is what separates an experiment from a wish.

Weak“Evaluate on more datasets” or “scale it up.” Neither has an outcome attached, so neither can be wrong.
StrongSpecific enough that someone could run it this week, and stated with both outcomes: what you expect, and what you would conclude if the opposite happened.

Collaboration

Discuss the papers freely. That is the point of a seminar. Write your response alone, and name anyone you discussed it with in the header. Doing so costs you nothing; its absence, when we can tell, costs a lot.

A worked example

On a paper that is not on the reading list, so that nobody's response is written for them. It is the paper the volumetric-fusion lecture builds from, and every assigned paper in Unit 1 is downstream of it.

Example response

Newcombe et al., KinectFusion: Real-Time Dense Surface Mapping and Tracking, ISMAR 2011.

1. The claim

A commodity depth camera and a GPU are enough to build a dense, drift-free model of a room-scale scene in real time, provided you fuse every frame into one persistent volume and track against that rather than against the previous frame. The rival being argued against is frame-to-frame odometry, which was the standard and which drifts.

2. The evidence

The comparison that carries the claim is frame-to-model against frame-to-frame ICP: it is the only result that separates the paper's actual contribution (tracking against an accumulated surface) from the two things that came free with the hardware (dense depth, GPU throughput). It is convincing on the drift question. It is weaker than it looks on reconstruction accuracy, which is shown qualitatively (renders and a few closeups) with no quantitative comparison against ground-truth geometry, so “how good is the model” is largely taken on the reader's eye.

3. The weakest assumption

That the scene is static. The TSDF update is a running weighted average over every observation of a voxel, which is only meaningful if all of those observations are of the same surface. Nothing in the formulation detects that they are not. A person walking through the scene does not produce an error the system can flag; it produces a smeared surface that is indistinguishable, downstream, from a real one. Everything built on the map (planning, collision checking) inherits that silently.

4. What you would run next

Record one sequence of a static room, then the same trajectory with a person walking a fixed path through it. Fuse both and measure surface error along that path as a function of how long the person occupied each region. I expect error to grow roughly with dwell time and not to recover, because the weighted average has no mechanism to forget. If instead it recovers once the person leaves, with later observations outvoting earlier ones, then the static-scene assumption is much softer than the formulation suggests, and the practical fix is a weighting schedule rather than explicit dynamic-object handling.

How it is graded

Each response is marked out of 3. 3: all four questions answered with judgement, the assumption is one the paper does not concede, the experiment has an outcome attached. 2: complete and correct, but one or more answers restate the paper rather than assess it. 1: submitted, largely summary. 0: not submitted, or written about a paper you did not read.

Two dropped responses across the term, no questions asked. Late responses are not accepted after the lecture, because the discussion is the point and the response is what makes it possible.
