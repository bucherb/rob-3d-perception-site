Student-led seminars

Four sessions, one at the end of each unit. Eight of you sign up for each one, bring a paper, walk the room through it, and then run the discussion. Everything else about the session is deliberately loose.

Sessions Sep 30, Nov 2, Nov 11, Dec 2 Eight slots per session Your turn about ten minutes Sign up Piazza

What the session is

Ninety minutes on the papers of the unit that just finished, run by you rather than by me. Eight people sign up to bring a paper and walk the room through it; everyone else comes and joins the discussion.

I keep a loose eye on the clock and otherwise stay out of the way.

Sign up for a slot

  • Eight slots per session, first come. A sign-up sheet goes up on Piazza before each session; adding your name is a commitment to be in the room that day.
  • Pick any paper from the reading list, or propose one of your own. Anything in the unit's area is fair game: a paper the list skipped, something from last month, a classic the lectures leaned on. Tell me what you have chosen when you sign up so two people do not bring the same paper.
  • Over the term, aim to take one slot. Four sessions and eight slots each is enough for everyone.

Your ten minutes

Roughly four minutes walking the room through the paper, then roughly six leading a discussion about it. Nobody is timing you to the second.

01Walk us through it

Show the paper itself on screen, or slides if you like making them, or the project page with its video. Whatever gets the idea across fastest.

The one thing to land clearly is the key contribution: what this paper does that was not there before, in your own words, stated so that someone could disagree with it. A figure and a result usually carry it.

No slide deck is required, and there is nothing to submit beforehand. If you do bring slides, three or four is plenty.

02Then lead the discussion

Use the rest of the response form as your agenda. You have already answered these questions in writing for other papers, so the room knows the shape of them:

  • The evidence. Which experiment actually carries the claim, and how far?
  • The weakest assumption. Where does the argument rest on something that might not hold, and what breaks if it fails?
  • What you would run next. One experiment, with what result would change your mind.

Bring your own answer to each, but the point is to get other people arguing rather than to deliver a verdict. Asking the room "does anyone believe Table 2?" is a perfectly good way to spend two minutes.

If you are there without a slot

Read what you can, come, and argue. The discussion is the session, and it is the part of the seminar grade that is not tied to your own paper. One good question about someone else's paper is worth more than sitting through all eight quietly.

How it is graded

Anton grades the seminar: 10% for the session you lead, 5% for your part in the discussion across all four. There is nothing to hand in and no rubric to write to. He is judging whether you engaged with the paper, not how polished the delivery was, so a clear statement of the contribution and a discussion that goes somewhere is what earns it.
